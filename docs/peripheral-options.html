What else can I plug into the Cheap Yellow Display?

The board looks like it has spare ports. It mostly doesn't — the display and touch controller have already claimed twelve GPIOs between them. What you actually get is three free pins. But two of those three form an I²C bus, and a bus is not a pin: it's a socket for dozens of devices at once.

Three traps that cost people an evening.

GPIO 22 appears on both P3 and CN1 — it is one pin, not two. Wiring something to it on each header shorts two devices onto the same line.

GPIO 35 is input-only and has no internal pull-up. It can read a sensor or a button, but it cannot drive an LED, a relay, or a chip-select, and a button on it needs an external resistor.

GPIO 27 is on ADC2, which is dead while WiFi is running. Harmless for I²C, which is digital — but never analogRead() it. That is the same reason the on-board light sensor sits on GPIO 34 (ADC1).

Why two pins is more than it sounds

The CN1 header carries GND, 3V3, SDA (27) and SCL (22) — a complete I²C bus with power. I²C devices are addressed, not wired individually, so they chain off the same four wires. Twenty sensors use the same two GPIOs as one. Your ceiling is address collisions and how much 3.3 V current the on-board regulator will give you, not pin count.

That reframes the question. It isn't "which one device do I add" — it's "what should this weather station know that it currently doesn't."

The physical gotcha: buy the cable first

CN1 and P3 are 1.25 mm pitch connectors (Molex PicoBlade, commonly sold as "JST 1.25"). Almost every sensor you'll buy terminates in 2.54 mm headers or a Qwiic/STEMMA QT plug (1.0 mm) — none of which fit. A 4-pin 1.25 mm pigtail is the single part that unblocks everything else, and it's the one people forget.

Sensors that make it a real weather station

BME280 I²C

Temperature, humidity and barometric pressure. The strongest single addition: pressure trend is the one genuine forecasting signal you can measure locally, and it lets the display compare indoor readings against the outdoor API numbers it already fetches. Watch for BMP280 look-alikes — those drop humidity.

~$8–15 Search Amazon →
DS3231 real-time clock I²C

Fixes a real weakness in the current build. Every clock on screen depends on NTP, so with no WiFi there is no time at all. A battery-backed DS3231 holds accurate time through outages and reboots — and it's temperature-compensated, so it drifts seconds per year rather than minutes per month.

~$7–12 Search Amazon →
BH1750 lux meter I²C

Also fixes something already flagged. The auto-brightness currently reads a bare LDR whose wiring polarity is an untested guess. The BH1750 reports calibrated lux over I²C — no polarity question, no guessing, and a far better curve for dimming at a bedside.

~$6–10 Search Amazon →
AS3935 lightning detector I²C

The one that makes people stop and look. Detects the RF signature of lightning and estimates distance to the storm front, typically out to ~40 km — genuinely ahead of any API, since it sees the strike before a forecast updates. Fussy about electrical noise; keep it away from the display ribbon.

~$25–40 Search Amazon →
SCD41 CO&sub2; sensor I²C

True NDIR carbon dioxide, not the "equivalent CO&sub2;" that cheap VOC parts estimate. Indoor CO&sub2; is the number that actually tracks stuffiness in a closed room, which is something no weather API can tell you about your own house.

~$45–65 Search Amazon →
LTR390 UV sensor I²C

Measured UV index at your window rather than a regional estimate. Small, cheap, and a natural fit next to the existing sunrise/sunset data the app already pulls.

~$10–18 Search Amazon →
PMSA003I particulate sensor I²C

PM2.5 / PM10 air quality. Worth it if wildfire smoke or seasonal air quality is something you track; it's physically the largest part here and needs airflow, so plan where it sits.

MLX90614 IR thermometer I²C

A non-contact thermometer pointed at the sky reads far colder on a clear night than an overcast one — so it doubles as a cloud-cover detector, and a decent frost predictor. A genuinely clever trick for the money.

Already soldered on — costs you nothing but code

microSD card slot on board

Wired to GPIO 5 plus the VSPI bus the touch controller already uses. Buy a card and you get history: log every reading and the display can show a 24-hour trend instead of only this instant. Note the shared bus — touch and SD must take turns, which is a code concern, not a wiring one.

~$7–12 (32 GB is plenty) Search Amazon →
Speaker on P4 on board

There's a class-D amplifier on GPIO 26 feeding the P4 connector. A small 8 Ω speaker gives you audible severe-weather alerts — the one thing a silent display genuinely can't do. Keep it small; the board offers no current limiting.

~$8–14 / pair Search Amazon →
RGB status LED on board

GPIO 4 / 16 / 17, active low. Free ambient signalling with zero hardware: a colour glance for storm warnings, fetch failures, or WiFi loss without lighting the whole panel at 2 am.

Settle it for about twelve dollars

Before committing to an expensive sensor, buy the pigtail and one cheap I²C part — a BME280 or BH1750 — and run an I²C scan on pins 27/22. If it prints an address, every ASSUMPTION row above is confirmed at once, and the rest of this list becomes a shopping decision rather than a gamble.

  • Check the silkscreen first. Read the labels next to the connectors on your actual board and compare against the table above before ordering.
  • Everything must be 3.3 V logic. The ESP32 is not 5 V tolerant; a 5 V module on SDA/SCL can damage the pin.
  • Mind the power budget. The 3V3 pin comes off a small on-board regulator that is already feeding the ESP32, the backlight and the panel. Low-current sensors are fine; anything with a heater, a fan, or a motor needs its own supply.
  • Watch for address collisions. Two devices at the same I²C address need one of them changed by jumper, or an I²C multiplexer.
